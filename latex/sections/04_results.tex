\subsection{Sample and scoring}
\label{sec:results:sample}

The analysed corpus comprises \ncells{} scored cells on the $[0,1]$ composite,
of which \ncellsen{} are English-prompt responses across the 25 countries; the
remainder are the within-country native-language pairs used for H2. Code
adjudicates the factual verdict against the official register wherever the
answer is a published number, resolving \ncodecovtone\% of T1,
\ncodecovttwo\% of T2 and \ncodecovtthree\% of T3 cells; the residual, with
all of T4, is scored by the mean of a three-vendor panel, and T5 retains the
scores of the collection judge. In total \ncodecells{} cells carry a code or
panel score.

The panel mean replaces the single-judge design of the original protocol, in
which the collection judge (GPT-5-mini for the pre-specified fifteen countries,
Claude Haiku~4.5 for the ten extension countries) was itself among the
evaluated models and, because the extension holds seven of the ten Global North
countries, was confounded with tier. Judges differ in severity (mean composite
$\njudgegemini$ for Gemini, $\njudgeclaude$ for Claude, $\njudgedeepseek$ for
DeepSeek), so a single judge fixes an arbitrary level, and single-judge
reliability is ICC$(2,1)=0.56$ against ICC$(2,3)=0.79$ for the panel mean. One
panel member (DeepSeek-V3) is also an evaluated model, so we checked for
self-favouring: it scores its own outputs more harshly relative to the other
two judges ($-0.177$) than it scores any other model's ($-0.123$ to $-0.160$).

\subsection{Accuracy by model}
\label{sec:results:model}

Table~\ref{tab:conf-model} orders the 14 models by mean composite: frontier
systems lead and small open-weight models trail. On H3, the Brazilian-Portuguese
regional model Cabra-Mistral~7B is the weakest of all ($\ncabra$ against
$\nrestmean$ for the rest; Mann-Whitney $p\approx 0$ on cells, Cliff's
$\delta=\nregdelta$). Cliff's $\delta$ is the probability that a randomly drawn
answer from one group beats one from the other, rescaled to $[-1,+1]$, so
$\nregdelta$ means the regional model loses roughly three such comparisons for
every one it wins. With the country as the unit, Cabra trails the mean of the
other thirteen in \nhthreebyctryneg{} of 25 countries by $\nhthreebyctry$~pp
on average (Wilcoxon $p=\nhthreebyctryp$). The pre-specified test was narrower,
Cabra against its scale-matched peer Llama~3.1~8B on Portuguese-language
prompts, and it points the same way with a smaller margin: on Brazil the
regional model scores $\nhthreebra$ against $\nhthreebrallama$
($\delta=\nhthreebradelta$, $n=20$ cells each), and across the three
Portuguese-speaking countries $\nhthreelus$ against $\nhthreeluslama$
($\delta=\nhthreelusdelta$, $n=60$ each); paired by prompt with the replicates
averaged, Cabra loses on \nhthreenarrowneg{} of the \nhthreenarrown{}
Portuguese prompts, by $\nhthreenarrow$~pp (Wilcoxon $p=\nhthreenarrowp$).
Two readings of this have to be separated, and only one of them is a
finding. That a 7B model trails frontier systems an order of magnitude larger is
a statement about scale. The pre-specified question was narrower and is
answerable: does tuning a base model on Brazilian Portuguese improve what it
recalls about Brazil? Re-served on the same 30 Portuguese items, Cabra and the
base model it was fine-tuned from return the register value at
\ncabsanitycabra\% and \ncabsanitybase\% (Section~\ref{sec:results:robust}).
Regional instruction tuning at this scale does not improve register-value
recall over its own base, and the composite margin against Llama~3.1~8B comes
from the judged tasks and subcomponents rather than from the values the code
checks.

\subsection{Accuracy by task: the recall floor}
\label{sec:results:task}

Difficulty is task-dependent (Table~\ref{tab:conf-task}). The hardest task is
T1 (technical-standard recall, \ntasktone), which requires returning the
national annual PM\textsubscript{2.5} standard in force. The open
recommendation task (T5) scores highest (\ntasktfive) because its rubric
rewards plausible, well-structured policy advice that does not hinge on a
single fact. Pooling the two recall tasks (T1, T2; mean $\nfloorrecall$)
against synthesis and recommendation (T3--T5, $\nfloorrest$), the difference is
large on cells (Mann-Whitney $p\approx 0$, Cliff's $\delta=\nfloordelta$,
roughly two and a half losses for every win) and holds with the model as the
unit (recall below the rest in \nfloorbymodelneg{} of 14 models, by
$\nfloorbymodel$~pp, Wilcoxon $p=\nfloorbymodelp$) and with the country as the
unit (\nfloorbyctryneg{} of 25, $\nfloorbyctry$~pp, $p=\nfloorbyctryp$).
Models are weakest where applied environmental management needs them most, in
recalling the regulatory threshold in force.

The two corrections of the scoring are informative here. T2 was originally
scored against a placeholder key and rises from $0.368$ to $0.473$ once each
returned concentration is compared with the authorised range by code. T1 always
had an official key but was scored by two collection judges of different
severity, and moving its factual verdict to code raises it from $0.367$ to
$\ntasktone$ while keeping it the hardest task; the code verdict also shows
what the judges could not, that more than a third of the wrong T1 answers
(\nladderhit{} of \nincorrectn) cite a value that sits on the country's
official ladder, a superseded value or a future stage, rather than a fabricated
one (Section~\ref{sec:results:t1}). The floor is shallower than first reported
and concentrated in normative recall.

\subsection{H2: asking in the local language lowers accuracy}
\label{sec:results:h2}

For the nine countries with a target native language, each prompt is rendered
in both English and the native language with content held fixed, so that the
contrast is within-country and within-model. Replicates are averaged within
each (model, prompt) cell before pairing, since pairing replicate to replicate
would inflate $n$. Across the 839 matched cells, native-language prompting
lowers mean composite accuracy by \nnativa{} percentage points (Wilcoxon
signed-rank $p=\nnativap$). That test treats cells as independent, and they are
nested in nine countries and fourteen models, so we also report the inference
that respects the nesting: a mixed model on the paired differences with a
random intercept per country gives $\nhtwomixedcountry$~pp (SE
$\nhtwomixedcountryse$, $p=\nhtwomixedcountryp$), with a random intercept per
model $\nhtwomixedmodel$~pp ($p=\nhtwomixedmodelp$); with the country as the
unit ($n=9$) the penalty is negative in \nhtwocneg{} of nine and averages
$\nhtwocmean\pm\nhtwocse$~pp ($t=\nhtwoct$, $p=\nhtwoctp$); with the model as
the unit ($n=14$) it is negative in \nhtwomneg{} of fourteen (Wilcoxon
$p=\nhtwomwp$). All three languages carry a significant penalty
(Table~\ref{tab:h2}): Hindi $\nnativahi$~pp, Spanish $\nnativaes$~pp,
Portuguese $\nnativapt$~pp. Hindi is the language with the smallest corpus and
carries the largest penalty; Spanish and Portuguese, whose corpora differ in
size, carry penalties that do not differ. India, which scores highest of all 25
countries in English, drops furthest in Hindi, and part of that drop may be
regression to the mean, since a country whose English score is highest partly
by chance has further to fall; with a single Hindi country the two readings
cannot be separated.

H2 carries the study's central claim, and it survives every attempt to remove
it: it is not driven by any one country (leave-one-out $\nloocmin$ to
$\nloocmax$~pp, all significant), by any one model ($\nloommin$ to
$\nloommax$~pp), or by outlying cells (5\% trimmed $\ntrim$~pp, $p=\ntrimp$);
it holds under every weighting of the composite; and it appears in every task
and under both persona conditions. Nor is it silence. Native-language prompts
return an empty response more often than English ones, so the penalty could in
principle be a count of answers that never came; dropping every cell whose
stored response was empty leaves it at $\nhtwonoempty$~pp
($n=\nhtwonoemptyn$ pairs, $p=\nhtwonoemptyp$), marginally larger rather than
smaller. The penalty is what the model says, not what it fails to say.

Two further checks answer the objections a sceptical reader raises first. The
first is that language-model judges might penalise non-English prose for style
rather than content, so we test a binary outcome no judge touches: does the
response contain a value the extractor can compare with the register? The
extractor reads Devanagari numerals, decimal commas and units written out in
words, so a Hindi or a Spanish-language number is not penalised for its
notation (Section~\ref{sec:methods}). English prompts yield a verifiable value
$\nverifen\%$ of the time and native-language prompts $\nverifnat\%$, a paired
difference of $\nverifdiff$~pp, with the native version worse in \nverifworse{}
of the \nverifdisc{} discordant pairs (sign test $p=\nverifp$): Hindi
$\nverifhi$~pp, Spanish $\nverifes$~pp, while Portuguese, the best-resourced of
the three, shows no such loss ($\nverifpt$~pp). Portuguese responses return a
number as readily as English ones; the number is more often wrong. On the code
verdict itself (native minus English, cells adjudicated by code in both
languages) the Portuguese penalty is $\nhtwocodept$~pp ($n=\nhtwocodeptn$,
$p=\nhtwocodeptp$) and the Spanish one $\nhtwocodees$~points
($n=\nhtwocodeesn$, not significant), $\nhtwocodeall$~pp overall
($n=\nhtwocodealln$, $p=\nhtwocodeallp$). Where the language is scarce the
model is less likely to return a checkable number at all, and where the
language is well resourced the number it returns is less often right.

The second is that the native prompts, produced by an LLM translator, might ask
something different. We tested this on all 90 unique native prompts,
back-translating each with a different model and having two further models
audit the result against the English original. On the two items that could
confound the effect, fidelity is complete: no prompt changed the country and
none changed the requested quantity ($90/90$ on both). Restricting H2 to the
prompts verified as faithful leaves it unchanged ($\nfaithful$~pp,
$p=\nfaithfulp$) against $\ndivergent$~pp among the divergent ones, a
difference not distinguishable from zero (permutation $p=\nfidelityp$).
Separating the pairs by the instrument that scored the native cell, the penalty
is $\nhtwocode$~pp where code decided ($n=\nhtwocoden$, $p=\nhtwocodep$),
$\nhtwopanel$~pp where the panel decided ($n=\nhtwopaneln$, $p=\nhtwopanelp$)
and $\nhtwoorig$~pp, not significant, where the collection judge's score
remains ($n=\nhtwoorign$, essentially T5, the open recommendation with no
register value). The code-adjudicated subset is the smaller of the two because
code acts only where a number exists, so it conditions on the model having
produced a value in both languages and selects away the severest failure mode;
the panel subset carries that failure mode, which is why judge leniency cannot
be assumed to cancel across languages and why the judge-free outcome above is
the one that settles the point.

The applied implication runs against intuition. A manager who suspects an
English-language model serves their country poorly, and responds by asking in
the national language, makes the answer worse, and worse by the largest margin
where the language is least represented in training corpora.

\subsection{H6: persona does not narrow the geographic gap}
\label{sec:results:h6}

The persona factor was fully crossed, so H6 is tested as a
difference-in-differences. The public-environmental-manager frame leaves the
North/South gap unchanged: $\ndidneutral$~pp under the neutral frame against
$\ndidmanager$~pp under the manager frame, a DiD of $\ndid$~pp, far below the
pre-specified 5~pp threshold; a country-level permutation test (5{,}000
permutations of the tier label) gives $p=\npersonap$. The pre-specified test
cannot distinguish absence of an effect from lack of power, so we add the
equivalence test: the country-level bootstrap 90\% interval of the DiD is
$\ndidcinety$~pp, inside both the $\pm5$~pp margin of the hypothesis and a
stricter $\pm2$~pp margin, so that the frame is equivalent to no frame within
2~pp on the gap. The persona has a main effect of its own, and it is negative:
paired by prompt and model, the manager frame lowers the composite by
$\npersonamain$~pp ($n=\npersonamainn$ pairs, Wilcoxon $p=\npersonamainp$; with
the country as the unit $p=\npersonamainctryp$). H6 is not supported:
presenting the query as coming from a public environmental manager neither
narrows nor amplifies the Global South disadvantage, consistent with evidence
that expert personas do not improve factual
accuracy~\citep{zheng2024helpful,mollick2025personas}. A manipulation check
finds that $50.2\%$ of persona-condition responses explicitly acknowledge the
role frame, so the null is not an artefact of the frame being ignored.

\subsection{H1: a tier gap that holds, a gradient that does not}
\label{sec:results:h1}

H1 was pre-specified in two parts, a development gradient as the primary test
and the binary Global North/South contrast as its sensitivity analysis, and the
two separate cleanly.

The primary outcome for the contrast is the one no judge touches. On the
code-adjudicated outcomes (all of T1 and the resolved part of T2 and T3), Global
North countries return the register value $\ncodegn$ of the time and Global
South countries $\ncodegs$, a gap of $\ncodegap$~pp with a country-level
bootstrap interval of $\ncodegapci$ (permutation of the tier label with the
country as the unit, $p=\ncodegapp$). On the composite, the secondary outcome, the ten Global North
countries average $\naccgn$ against $\naccgs$ for the fifteen Global South
countries, a gap of $\ntiergap$ percentage points whose 95\% bootstrap interval
over countries is $\ntiergapci$~pp (permutation $p=\ntiergapperm$). The
composite gap is stable to dropping any single country (leave-one-out
$[\nloogapmin,\nloogapmax]$~pp) and to the composite weighting
($\ngapequal$~pp under equal weights, $\ngapfactual$~pp on the factual
subcomponent, where it widens). Treating the four European Union members in the
sample (Germany, France, Italy, Portugal) as a single unit, so that shared
regulation is not counted four times, leaves it at $\ngapeu$~pp
$\ngapeuci$ ($p=\ngapeup$, \ngapeuunits{} Northern units against 15). Because
seven of the ten Global North countries entered with the extension, we also
report the gap where the wave cannot explain it: within the extension alone
(seven Global North against Colombia, Chile and Angola) it is
$\ntiergapwave$~pp $\ntiergapwaveci$, and on the pre-specified fifteen (three
Global North against twelve) $\ntiergappre$~pp $\ntiergappreci$, a point
estimate of the same size on a sample too small to make it significant by
permutation ($p=\ntiergapprep$). Coverage is uneven across models, and that
unevenness is the gap's weakest point. Restricting the estimate to the
\ngapbalprompts{} of \ngapbaltotal{} English prompts that every one of the 14
models answered leaves the point estimate unchanged ($\ngapbal$~pp) and the
interval, on far fewer cells, includes zero ($\ngapbalci$, $p=\ngapbalp$). The
composite gap's significance therefore depends on the unbalanced cells: the
effect does not reverse or shrink when coverage is equalised, but this sample
cannot demonstrate it on a balanced design. The code-adjudicated contrast above,
which pools tasks rather than prompts, is the one that holds with the country as
the unit.

\emph{Where the gap lives.} The decomposition by task was not pre-specified and
is exploratory. Re-estimating the contrast on each task separately, with the
outcome coded as returning the register value, shows the disadvantage scaling
with how much the task depends on a fact specific to that country
(Table~\ref{tab:tier-by-task}). The table gives two estimates of each odds
ratio. The binomial mixed model with crossed random intercepts for country and
model returns a conditional odds ratio with a narrow interval; the country-level
bootstrap of the raw odds ratio, which treats the country as the unit of
inference, returns a wider one and is the honest measure of what 24 countries
can establish per task. On the binding national standard (T1), scored by code
against the instrument in force, the raw Global South odds of returning the
register value are $\norrawone$ of the Global North's ($\nortgn\%$ against
$\nortgs\%$), with a country-level interval of $\norrawoneci$ and a
country-level permutation $p=\norrawonep$; the conditional estimate is
$\nortone$ $\nortoneci$. On policy instruments (T4) the raw ratio is
$\norrawfour$ $\norrawfourci$ and on health-evidence synthesis (T3), where the
answer is an international estimate rather than a national one, it rises to
$\norrawthree$ $\norrawthreeci$. Open recommendation (T5) is at ceiling in both
tiers ($\nortfivegn\%$ against $\nortfivegs\%$), so no contrast is estimable
there and the task is reported without an odds ratio. The T1 ratio holds its
direction under the two objections its composition invites
(Section~\ref{sec:results:t1}): setting aside the three Global South countries
with no national standard the conditional estimate is $\nortexcl$
$\nortexclci$, and on the pre-specified fifteen it is $\nortpre$ $\nortpreci$.
T4 is the one task in the table scored entirely by the panel, so its ratio
could carry a judge's own ignorance of Southern instruments as well as the
model's; the code-only estimates are the ones free of that.

Two readings of this table have to be kept apart. The point estimates order
themselves by how much the answer depends on a fact about the country, which is
the pattern the paper's argument predicts, and the conditional model returns
narrow intervals for each of them. But the conditional interval is an interval
for a within-country, within-model contrast: the country intercepts alone have
a standard deviation of $\nsdctryone$ on T1 and $\nsdctryfour$ on T4
(Supplementary Material), so the countries are far from exchangeable and a
reader who wants a statement about countries needs the country-level interval.
Every one of those includes~1 (Figure~\ref{fig:or-task}). We therefore report
the per-task ordering as a pattern of point estimates and the pooled
code-adjudicated contrast as the finding: the gap is real in the aggregate and
its concentration on country-specific tasks is exploratory.

below the pre-specified $\rho\geq0.55$ threshold, which is the criterion H1 was

one of the size we committed to. At the pre-specified $n=15$ it is absent

disadvantage is established as a difference between groups, while its shape

primary family $\{$H1-gradient, H4, H6$\}$, no test rejects its null.

(Figure~\ref{fig:countries}): India ($\nindia$, Global South) tops all
countries, Indonesia and South Africa outrank most of the Global North, and
Canada and France sit low. The disadvantage is a tier difference layered over
substantial country-specific variation, and that heterogeneity is part of why
the gradient does not resolve.

\subsection{H4: corpus representation is not established}
\label{sec:results:h4}

The pre-specified mechanism was training-corpus representation, proxied by
Wikipedia article and edition count. That proxy is null: Spearman
$\rho=\nhfourwiki$ ($p=\nhfourwikip$) uncontrolled, and still null partialling
out HDI and log GDP per capita. A post-hoc country-coverage proxy reaches
$\rho=\nhfoursite$ ($p=\nhfoursitep$) and attenuates after adjusting for HDI
(partial $\rho=\nhfourpartial$, $p=\nhfourpartialp$). At country level neither
channel is established, because with 25 countries coverage and development are
collinear ($\rho=\nrhocobhdi$ between them).

\subsubsection*{Changing the unit of variation, and what it can and cannot show}

Each country answers five tasks that differ in how much the answer depends on
a fact about that country: T1, T2 and T4 do; T3 (an international WHO estimate)
and T5 (a generic recommendation) do not. Countries score $\nhfourdep$ on the
first block and $\nhfourind$ on the second, a specificity deficit of
$\nhfourdeficit$. If what limits the model is how much the corpus says about a
given country, that deficit should be smaller where coverage is greater, and a
fixed effect for country absorbs every country-level main effect, development
and income included, though not their interaction with task dependence. At the
response level the interaction between coverage and task dependence is
$\beta=\nhfourbeta$ (SE $\nhfourse$, $p=\nhfourp$, $n=\nhfourn$), and the
pattern across classifications is the one a real effect would produce
(Table~\ref{tab:h4-within}): the purest contrast, T1 against T5, yields the
largest coefficient, dropping T5 or T4 leaves it intact, and a placebo
inverting the labels returns the exact mirror image.

That response-level $p$-value is not the evidence, because the interaction
varies only between 25 countries, and at the country level the two candidate
exposures separate. With standard errors clustered by country, coverage carries
$p=\nhfourclp$; development carries $\beta=\nhfourhdialonebeta$ with
$p=\nhfourhdialoneclp$. The 25 country-level deficits say the same: the
deficit correlates with HDI at $\rho=\nhfourctryrhohdi$
($p=\nhfourctryphdi$) and with coverage at $\rho=\nhfourctryrho$
($p=\nhfourctryp$), falling to $\rho=\nhfourctrypartial$
($p=\nhfourctrypartialp$) once HDI is partialled out. Entered together the two
are uninformative, because they correlate at $\rho=\nrhocobhdi$ and neither
survives (coverage $\beta=\nhfourjcob$, $p=\nhfourjcobclp$; HDI
$\beta=\nhfourjhdi$, $p=\nhfourjhdiclp$); within the nine anglophone countries,
where the language of the question is the same by construction, the coverage
coefficient is $\nhfouranglo$ with $p=\nhfouranglclp$ on nine clusters. The
language-corpus proxy entered alongside coverage is null at every level
($\beta=\nhfourlang$, $p=\nhfourlangp$), as predicted for responses elicited in
English.

What survives with the country as the unit is therefore the development
interaction, not the coverage one. The result is narrower than it sounds, and
it is not the gradient of H1: a country's development level does not predict how
accurately models answer about it overall (Section~\ref{sec:results:h1}), but it
does predict how much worse they answer the questions whose answer is a fact
about that country. Coverage points the same way and does not clear the
threshold, and the two cannot be separated in a sample where they correlate at
$\nrhocobhdi$. We report the pre-specified corpus mechanism as not established,
the development interaction as the one country-level mechanism result that
clears $0.05$, and the choice between the two exposures as open.

\subsection{H5: open versus closed-accessible}
\label{sec:results:h5}

Closed-accessible models average $\nhfive$~pp above open-weight ones, against
the optimistic H5 framing. The open arm includes larger models, so the gap is
not attributable to scale alone, although DeepSeek-V3 ranks first overall. H5
is interpreted descriptively.

\subsection{Ground-truth provenance, and what the T1 key decides}
\label{sec:results:gt}
\label{sec:results:t1}

Every key comes from an official register, anchored by documentary
verification and reproducible from the cited URLs: T1 from the national
regulation, T2 from WHO Ambient Air Quality Database v6.1, T3 from WHO GHO
indicator AIR\_41, T4 from the UNEP Global Assessment of Air Pollution
Legislation. Three countries have no national PM\textsubscript{2.5} standard,
confirmed from the official text (Nigeria regulates PM\textsubscript{10} only,
Argentina has no binding federal value, Angola no national legislation), which
is where a faithful model must decline to state a non-existent threshold. Egypt
has no retrievable value and therefore no T1 key. All four of these cases are
Global South, so the composition of the key is itself a candidate explanation
for the T1 gap. It is not: set the three no-standard countries aside and the
conditional Global South odds on T1 are $\nortexcl$ $\nortexclci$ of the Global
North's, against $\nort$ with them. The key also moved under review, in the
Southern direction the argument of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} predicts:
Bangladesh's entry carried the 15~$\mu$g/m\textsuperscript{3} of its 2005
gazette until an external reader pointed to the 2022 Rules, which set 35, and
with the corrected key Bangladesh's T1 accuracy falls from $0.39$ to $\nbgdt$,
because most models cite the superseded value. The full registry is in
Supplementary Material. Wikipedia and Wikidata appear only as the explanatory
variable of the mechanism test; no answer is scored against them.

\emph{The key in force, and the ladder around it.} Several instruments set a
ladder of values rather than one: Brazil's CONAMA 506/2024 runs 20, 17, 15, 10,
5~$\mu$g/m\textsuperscript{3} across five stages, of which 17 has been in force
since January 2025; the European limit is 25 with 10 from 2030; the United
Kingdom's limit value is 20 with an English target of 10 by 2040; the United
States moved from 12.0 to 9.0 in 2024; Bangladesh from 15 to 35 in 2022. The
T1 key is the value in force in the collection window, and it is exacting: no
model returns 17 for Brazil (the modal answer is 15, the next stage), almost
none returns 20 for the United Kingdom (most return the 2040 target or the
superseded 25), and fewer than a quarter return 9.0 for the United States. Of
the \nincorrectn{} wrong T1 answers, \nladderhit{} cite a value that sits
somewhere on the country's official ladder, a stage not yet in force or a value
since replaced, which is confusion about which value binds rather than
fabrication, and is the failure a training cut-off would produce. The
distinction does not rescue the tier comparison; it sharpens it. Scoring any
ladder value as correct raises Global North accuracy on T1 from $\nstrictgn$ to
$\nladdergn$ and Global South accuracy only from $\nstrictgs$ to $\nladdergs$,
because the staged instruments are mostly in the North, and the conditional
odds ratio falls to $\nortladder$ $\nortladderci$. Where the register offers
several plausible numbers the models know them; where it offers one, they
often do not.

\subsection{Qualitative failure modes}
\label{sec:results:qual}

Three failure modes recur behind the composite, with verbatim examples in
Supplementary Material. Models fabricate standards that do not exist for the
three countries with no national norm: in \nnsfab{} of the \nnscells{} scored T1
cells for Angola, Argentina and Nigeria the answer asserts a specific national
value, in \nnsabst{} it correctly states that none exists, and in \nnsdk{} it
declines to answer, and the invented values disagree among replicates. They miss
the binding value, answering 15~$\mu$g/m\textsuperscript{3} for Brazil where
the stage in force is 17 (CONAMA 506/2024), a confusion of stage rather than an
invention. And they degrade in the native language, as when GPT-OSS~120B
returned India's correct 40~$\mu$g/m\textsuperscript{3} in English and an
empty answer in Hindi.

\subsection{Inter-judge reliability}
\label{sec:results:reliability}

Reliability is reported on the sample that produces the effects, not on a
calibration subset. All 3{,}190 items requiring a judge were scored by all three
panel members, giving Krippendorff's $\alpha=\nalpha$, single-judge
ICC$(2,1)=\nicc$, and, as the operative quantity since the analysis uses the
panel mean, ICC$(2,3)=\niccpanel$.

The panel also isolates a design rule that generalises beyond this study. Where
the judging prompt asked the model to compare a returned concentration against
a tolerance band expressed in words, that is, to perform the arithmetic itself,
inter-judge concordance on those items was $r=-0.04$. Pre-computing the
admissible range and asking only whether the value falls inside it raised
concordance on the same items to $r=+1.00$, and the 274 scores collected under
the ambiguous wording were discarded and recollected. Apparent judge
disagreement can be an artefact of what the judge is asked to compute, which
is the same lesson \citet{zheng2023judging} draw when a reference answer cuts
a judge's failure rate on arithmetic from 70\% to 15\%. Arithmetic belongs to
code.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:results:robust}

The three supported effects were tested against seven attacks. The
native-language penalty and the recall floor survive all of them; the tier gap
survives five and is marginal under two, which the next paragraph states
exactly. The development gradient stays below the pre-specified $0.55$ under
every one (its highest value, $\nloorhomaxabs$, is a leave-one-out re-estimate).
Full outputs are in Supplementary Material.

\emph{No single country, and no weighting, carries the result.}
Leave-one-country-out re-estimation holds the composite tier gap in
$[\nloogapmin,\nloogapmax]$~pp and the native-language penalty at $\nloocmin$
to $\nloocmax$~pp. Under equal weights the gap is $\ngapequal$~pp and the
language penalty $\nnativaequal$~pp; restricting to factual accuracy against
the register value widens them to $\ngapfactual$ and $\nnativafactual$~pp and
deepens the recall floor, so that both effects grow where the measurement is
most objective. The two replicates of each cell differ by $\nrepdiff$~pp on
average (within-cell SD $\nrepsd$~pp), so the $\nnativasigned$~pp language
penalty, averaged over 839 pairs, stands roughly ten standard errors from the
noise two replicates leave unresolved.

\emph{Where the tier gap is established and where it is marginal.}
Specifications of one dataset are not independent tests, so we state each and
what it assumes rather than counting them. The finding is the pooled
code-adjudicated contrast, where no judge contributes and the country is the
unit ($\ncodegap$~pp, $\ncodegapci$, permutation $p=\ncodegapp$). On the
composite, a permutation test over the 25 country means gives
$p=\ntiergapperm$ and a Bayesian hierarchical model with crossed random
intercepts agrees on sign and magnitude ($\beta=\nbayes$, 94\% HDI
$[\nbayeslo,\nbayeshi]$, $P(\beta<0)=\nbayesp$), while the frequentist mixed
model, which is the specification that models the unbalanced design correctly,
leaves it marginal ($\beta=\nmixedbeta$, $p=\nmixedp$) because it charges the
large between-country heterogeneity to the error term, and the balanced-prompt
subset gives $p=\ngapbalp$. The gap is therefore supported on the code-only
outcome and on the composite by permutation, and marginal under the mixed model
and on the balanced subset. Its E-value is $\nevalue$ at the point estimate and
$\nevaluelim$ at the confidence limit nearest the
null~\citep{vanderweele2017evalue}: a country-level confounder of modest
strength would suffice to explain it away.

\emph{The gap follows the UNCTAD partition, not the HDI partitions.}
Re-estimated under three development-based partitions of the same 25
countries, the gap is significant under UNCTAD and non-significant under every
split drawn on HDI (Supplementary Material). This is consistent with the null
HDI gradient, since a split on HDI should not then be expected to produce a
gap, and it concerns the accuracy level rather than the country-specificity
deficit, where development is the exposure that does clear the threshold
(Section~\ref{sec:results:h4}). One reading of the first is that position in
the world system rather than income separates the accuracy
level~\citep{quijano2000coloniality,mohamed2020decolonial}; the reading is post
hoc, and we claim the gap for the North/South distinction as conventionally
drawn, not as a general statement about development.

\emph{What the null gradient does and does not exclude.} Simulating 25-country
samples at known correlations, the design has $\npower\%$ power at
$\rho=0.55$, the threshold fixed in advance, and a minimum detectable effect
of $\rho=\nmde$. Power says how often a true gradient of that size would have
been detected; it does not make the observed interval narrower, and the
interval reported above still includes $0.55$. The ten countries added post hoc
fall within the HDI range the original fifteen already spanned, so the
extension raises the density of observations rather than the range of the
predictor.

\emph{The regional model was served as published.} Because H3 rests on one
locally served model, we verified the serving stack after the fact. The Ollama
template used for Cabra-Mistral~7B is the chat template published with the
model's tokenizer, and the model answers the Portuguese prompts in coherent
Portuguese. We then re-served the 30 Portuguese prompts, with two replicates,
to Cabra, to its declared base (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 at the same
quantisation) and to Llama~3.1~8B, and scored T1--T3 with the study's code
extractor (Supplementary Material). \ncabrasanitysentence{}
